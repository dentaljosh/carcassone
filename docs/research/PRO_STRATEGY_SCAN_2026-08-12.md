# Pro/Expert Human Carcassonne Strategy Scan — 2026-08-12

**Status:** current (research scan, not yet actioned)
**Purpose:** mine expert/strong-human Carcassonne strategy sources + anti-AI-in-other-games literature for candidate mechanisms to test against our archived human-vs-AI games. Companion to the observation that Joshua is now beating the champion 13–10 (+10 pts/game, n=23) head-to-head on the phone, and that our own EV-loss grader scores several of his winning moves as apparent blunders — implying the hand-tuned leaf evaluator has a blind spot a human can see.
**Scope discipline:** cross-checked every candidate below against the killed-lever list in the task brief (tile-counting terms, stranded-meeple penalty, deck-graded closure probability, merge-flip anticipation, category-convex meeple lock-up, targeted denial of near-complete large opponent cities, farm-growth bonus term, phase-aware eval weighting, deeper search / deeper endgame). Findings are tagged **NEW**, **COLLIDES**, or **UNCLEAR** accordingly.

**Method note on sourcing:** BoardGameGeek thread pages, Carcassonne Central, and wikicarpedia.com all return HTTP 403 to automated fetches (BGG bot-blocks; Carcassonne Central and wikicarpedia sit behind the Anubis anti-bot challenge) — this closed off the two most promising forums for verified high-ELO/tournament discussion. I substituted: (a) direct WebFetch of ~10 independent strategy-guide sites that *do* answer, (b) WebSearch snippet-mining of the blocked forums (titles + Google's indexed excerpt only, weaker evidence), (c) the peer-reviewed game-AI literature on Carcassonne MCTS and on determinization/PIMC failure modes, and (d) the anti-superhuman-AI literature in Go/chess/StarCraft. Where a claim rests only on a WebSearch snippet rather than a fetched page, that is flagged in the credibility line.

## Findings

### F1. Big/open cities are folk-wisdom-condemned as structurally inefficient — this is the flagged NEVER-TRIED item, and the consensus is a clear ENDORSE
**Mechanism:** Carcassonne city scoring gives no per-tile-size bonus (2 pts/tile + 2 pts/shield whether the city is 2 tiles or 12), while completion probability drops and steal/merge vulnerability rises as a city grows — every additional open edge is both a harder tile-matching requirement to close AND another door for an opponent to join for a tie/majority. Guidance converges on: build small (2–4 tile) cities, and when choosing which of two builders to expand, prefer starting shapes with one open edge, tolerate two, avoid three.
**Sources (independently-run guide sites, direct-fetched, all converging):**
- tilelord.com/strategy — "Build small cities (2-4 tiles only)... Other players will try to merge into large cities... you might not be able to complete (close) large cities."
- boostyourplay.com/carcassonne-the-ultimate-strategy-guide-16-pro-tips — "Cities don't scale in Carcassonne. You don't get any bonus points per tile for a larger city... large cities become targets for other players to share or steal and they become harder to complete as their borders grow larger."
- elusivemeeple.com/2018/07/08/carcassonne-strategy-tips — "watch out for bigger castles — they become takeover targets. The best way to avoid being taken over is to start with one that leads to a single edge – two edges is okay, three edges is bad."
- meeplemountain.com/articles/carcassonne-strategy-guide — "the bigger a city is the more likely it is that your opponents will sabotage or parasitize it."
**Credibility:** forum/blog consensus — four independent, non-affiliated guide sites converge on the identical claim without citing each other; no WC-player or verified-high-ELO attribution found directly, but this is the closest thing to unanimous folk consensus in the whole scan.
**NEW vs killed list:** **NEW — flag prominently per the brief.** "Penalize large open cities" is explicitly listed as never-tried. Note it is adjacent to but distinct from the two related killed items: "targeted denial of near-complete large opponent cities" (about *blocking a specific opponent's near-finished city*, found harmful/bounded-null) and "farm-growth bonus term" (a *farm* size bonus, found null). Neither killed test evaluated a general penalty on the *acting player's own* open-city size/edge-count as a leaf-eval term, independent of whose city it is or how close to done.
**Test:** for every city-claim decision in the 26 archived games, record final city size and open-edge-count at claim time for both AI and human; check whether human claims/joins are systematically smaller/lower-edge than the AI's, and whether the AI's own leaf eval assigns positive-and-growing value to large open cities that the EV-loss/oracle grader would mark down. Cheap complementary check: run a self-play ladder with a candidate open-edge-count penalty term (distinct in form from the killed farm-growth-bonus and the killed denial term) and see if it moves win rate — this is a genuinely new cell in the leaf-eval search space, not a re-run of a dead one.

### F2. Late majority-steal joins into an opponent's big investment are a named core 2p tactic
**Mechanism:** deliberately placing a meeple to force a tie or take majority on a feature (city or farm) the opponent has sunk many tiles into converts their labor into shared/your score, because tied majority pays *all* tied players full points. Framed explicitly as a mid-game meeple's best use, and as the single highest-leverage class of move in 2p.
**Sources:**
- kingpandagames.com/carcassonne-meeple-placement — "You can join a feature and force a tie or take majority. This is often the best use of a meeple in the mid-game... If an opponent has built a 10-tile city and you can connect your meeple to it and force shared scoring, that single placement could earn you 20+ points off their labor."
- chessandpoker.com/carcassonne-rules-and-strategy-guide — "it's a valid technique to try and 'farm' a city from two or maybe even 3 sides" (farm-side analogue of the same idea).
- meeplemountain.com — same "sabotage/parasitize" framing as F1.
**Credibility:** blog-guide consensus (3 independent sources), no WC attribution.
**NEW vs killed list:** **NEW.** This is the *offense* mirror of the killed "targeted denial" lever — killed was about preventing an opponent from finishing; this is about profitably *joining* what they're already finishing. Different action class, not measured.
**Test:** in the 26 archived games, isolate human moves that joined-late-for-tie/majority on an AI-leading feature; compare the EV-loss grader's verdict on those specific moves against the AI's actual top alternative at that node, and check whether "connect for tie on opponent-majority feature" ever appears as a top-3 candidate in the AI's own search output at comparable board states in self-play.

### F3. Time a contest to the opponent's meeple reserve, not just the board
**Mechanism:** a player low on unplaced followers structurally cannot respond to an aggressive join or a new contest — so the correct moment to start a farm/city fight is when the opponent's reserve is thin, independent of the raw board-state value of the contested feature.
**Sources:**
- kingpandagames.com/carcassonne-meeple-placement — "watch your opponents' supply. An opponent running low can't respond to an aggressive join. An opponent with a full hand can contest anything."
- elusivemeeple.com — "look for opportunities to get them to commit extra meeples to a fight between each other" (the baiting complement — provoke the opponent to spend down their reserve first).
**Credibility:** blog-guide, 2 independent sources.
**NEW vs killed list:** **NEW.** Distinct from "stranded-meeple penalty" (a penalty on meeples already stuck on an unfinishable feature) and from "category-convex meeple lock-up" (a shape/category term). This is a relative, opponent-reserve-conditioned aggression trigger — the eval would need to condition on the *opponent's* remaining-follower count when scoring a contest-or-concede decision, which none of the killed levers did.
**Test:** cross-reference every contested-feature decision point in the 26 games against both players' live meeple-reserve counts; check whether the human's contest/concede choices correlate with the AI's (opponent's) reserve level in a way the AI's own leaf eval is blind to (i.e., does the AI's eval of "should I contest this" ever change as a function of the *opponent's* reserve, holding board state fixed?).

### F4. Diversify farm claims across multiple smaller fields instead of concentrating on one contested farm
**Mechanism:** rather than escalating a single big farm fight, hold a secondary smaller/uncontested field claim as insurance against losing the main one — spreads variance instead of doubling down.
**Source:** chessandpoker.com/carcassonne-rules-and-strategy-guide — "The guide recommends building backup smaller fields within your territory as defensive insurance."
**Credibility:** single poster / single guide site — weakest-sourced finding in this scan, include with caution.
**NEW vs killed list:** **UNCLEAR/NEW.** Not the same axis as "farm-growth bonus term" (a size-scaling bonus); this is about the *number of separate field claims* held simultaneously, a portfolio-diversification framing that wasn't in the killed list, but the single-source strength is low.
**Test:** in self-play, compare realized farm-points-per-committed-meeple for games where a player split farm meeples across ≥2 disjoint fields vs concentrated them in one, controlling for game length; check if this is a real edge or noise before spending eval-design effort on it.

### F5. Monastery "freeloading" next to an opponent's actively-growing structure
**Mechanism:** placing a monastery adjacent to a city or road the opponent is actively extending means their own future placements passively advance your monastery toward completion "for free," independent of your own tile draws.
**Source:** meeplemountain.com/articles/carcassonne-strategy-guide — "Place a monastery next to an opponent's city and they'll help you complete your monastery whilst trying to finish their city."
**Credibility:** single source, blog tier — but a narrow, concrete, mechanically checkable claim.
**NEW vs killed list:** **NEW.** No killed lever addressed monastery placement conditioned on *predicted opponent activity* in the neighborhood.
**Test:** in self-play and the archived games, compare completion-turn-count for monastery placements adjacent to a subsequently-active opponent zone vs isolated placements; check whether the leaf eval's per-tile "monastery progress" term is blind to whose future moves are likely to fill the remaining slots.

### F6. Play the score differential, not just the board — leader plays low-variance, trailer takes contested lines even at negative naive-EV
**Mechanism:** general competitive-game logic (repeated independently across guides, not Carcassonne-specific insight): a leader should minimize variance and block the opponent's big scoring chances; a trailer should seek variance/contests even where naive expected value is worse, because only variance-seeking recovers a deficit.
**Sources:** convergent phrasing across theboardgamer.org (via search snippet, site itself unreachable — DNS failure), elusivemeeple.com-style guides, and general "adapt to game state" advice appearing in nearly every guide surveyed.
**Credibility:** forum/blog consensus, no direct WC attribution; the underlying logic is standard game theory, not a Carcassonne-specific discovery.
**NEW vs killed list:** **NEW** as a testable axis. Distinct from "phase-aware (early/mid/late) eval weighting" (bounded-null) — that killed lever conditioned on *turn number*; this conditions on *running score differential*, a different state variable entirely.
**Test:** bucket the human's move choices in the 26 games by running score differential at decision time; check whether his deviations from the AI's top choice concentrate when he's behind, and whether those deviations are measurably higher-variance (wider spread of plausible outcomes per the oracle) than the AI's chosen line. Separately check whether the AI's own leaf eval or search value estimate is score-differential-aware at all, or purely maximizes expected margin regardless of whether that margin is already winning or losing.

### F7. PIMC/determinization search structurally undervalues robust ("hedge") moves relative to modal-world-optimal moves — a live, peer-reviewed structural argument, adapted here to Carcassonne's chance (not adversarial-hidden-info) setting
**Mechanism:** the classical failure modes of Perfect Information Monte Carlo search — **strategy fusion** (search is run independently per determinized world and then aggregated, so the resulting root decision can be inconsistent with what's optimal for *any* individual world, let alone the true unresolved state) and **non-locality** (a move that's locally optimal in each sampled world can still be globally inferior because the aggregation ignores what a move is worth *across* the distribution of worlds, not just within one) — were established for card games with adversarial hidden information (Bridge). Our engine determinizes over the *future tile-draw order*, which is symmetric chance rather than an opponent's private hand, so the classic strategy-fusion story (an agent implicitly assumes it knows the opponent's hidden cards) does not transfer cleanly — **this caveat matters and should gate how much weight this gets.** What plausibly *does* transfer: each of the 8 determinizations finds its own best line assuming a specific future tile sequence is "known," and root-aggregation is closer to a vote among per-world clairvoyant optima than to a true non-clairvoyant-optimal policy. A human favoring the lower-variance, more-robust-across-plausible-futures move (rather than the move that's excellent in the modal-looking determinization but fragile elsewhere) could out-perform the engine specifically at decision points where the 8 determinizations disagree sharply.
**Sources:** Frank & Basin, "Search in games with incomplete information: A case study using Bridge card play" (1998, foundational); Long, Sturtevant, Buro & Furtak, "Understanding the Success of Perfect Information Monte Carlo Sampling in Game Tree Search" (AAAI 2010) — <https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~nathanst/papers/pimc.pdf> (fetch returned binary/unparsed PDF; content below relayed from independent WebSearch snippets of this and related papers, cross-checked against my own training-time familiarity with the Frank & Basin result, which is a standard citation in the imperfect-information game-AI literature).
**Credibility:** peer-reviewed / well-established in game-AI literature for the mechanism itself; **UNCLEAR/adapted** for Carcassonne specifically, since our determinized variable is chance, not opponent-hidden-info — flag this as a hypothesis requiring the caveat, not a transplanted proven result.
**Relationship to existing work — check before treating as new:** this overlaps conceptually with the project's own already-measured **"clairvoyance gap"** (referenced in docs/INDEX.md / measurement/level2 verdicts) — that existing measurement presumably targets *value overestimation* from search having implicit future-tile-knowledge within a determinization. F7 proposes a different, complementary angle: not value overestimation, but *action-selection distortion* from disagreement across the 8 determinizations at the root. **Read the existing clairvoyance-gap verdict doc before running new analysis here — this may already be characterized, wholly or partly.**
**Test:** for the human's winning moves that the EV-loss grader scores as apparent AI blunders, compute the variance/spread of the AI's per-determinization root-value estimates (across its 8 determinizations) for its own chosen move at that decision point. Check whether high cross-determinization disagreement predicts "human out-move" better than the raw AI-vs-human value gap does — i.e., is the AI's blind spot concentrated at high-disagreement nodes specifically, distinguishable from a generic evaluator-accuracy story?

### F8. Anti-superhuman-AI pattern from other games: locally-safe-looking structures that are globally wrong, and failure to recognize a plan is failing
**Mechanism (background, not itself a Carcassonne claim):** in every major game where humans have beaten superhuman search+network engines, the exploit has one of two shapes — (a) a geometrically/structurally distinct pattern the value function rates as safe because it never saw enough of that shape in training/search (KataGo's "cyclic group" stone formations, rated nearly indestructible by the value net but slowly stranglable — Kellin Pelrine beat KataGo at 100k+ visits this way, >97% win rate for the adversarial policy class per the ICML paper); or (b) failure to adapt when a committed plan is visibly trending badly (Grzegorz "MaNa" Komincz's win over AlphaStar exploited AlphaStar's tendency to keep executing a strategy — building/unit placement patterns — after the position had turned, a flaw the DeepMind team itself later addressed by training explicit "exploiter" agents against the main policy).
**Sources:** humancompatible.ai/blog/2023/07/28/even-superhuman-go-ais-have-surprising-failure-modes/; far.ai/news/even-superhuman-go-ais-have-surprising-failure-modes/ (Wang et al., "Adversarial Policies Beat Superhuman Go AIs," ICML 2023, arXiv:2211.00241); TechSpot report on MaNa vs AlphaStar (techspot.com/news/78431).
**Credibility:** high — peer-reviewed (Go) and DeepMind-corroborated tech press (StarCraft); zero direct Carcassonne specificity, presented as motivating background for F7 and F9, not a standalone testable claim on its own.
**NEW vs killed list:** N/A (not a Carcassonne mechanism per se).

### F9. Hypothesis (my own synthesis, not directly sourced): the AI may not recognize a losing farm/city contest and pivot
**Mechanism:** applying F8's "fails to adapt when a plan is trending badly" pattern to Carcassonne specifically — once the AI has committed a meeple to a farm- or city-majority contest, does its eval recognize a multi-turn negative trend (opponent's advantage growing) and redeploy, or does it keep reinforcing/defending because each individual placement still looks locally positive relative to abandoning the sunk meeple? If the human's decisive wins came from farm/city contests the AI misjudged as still-favorable for several turns running, this would directly explain "human wins that the AI's own grader calls blunders."
**Credibility:** **UNCLEAR — this is my inference from the anti-AI literature applied to this project's specific symptom, not a sourced human-strategy claim.** Flag accordingly; treat as a hypothesis to check, not a finding to cite externally.
**NEW vs killed list:** NEW as a specific horizon-effect hypothesis about multi-turn contested-feature trend recognition — distinct from "phase-aware weighting" (turn-number-conditioned) and from any of the single-decision-point killed levers.
**Test:** directly on the 26 archived games — for every farm/city majority contest, reconstruct the AI's own turn-by-turn eval of "who's winning this contest"; check for contests where the trend was clearly negative for the AI's side across several turns before its eval caught up, and whether the human's decisive moves cluster at exactly those late-recognition windows. This is the single most directly diagnostic test in this report because it uses only data already in hand (no new self-play needed).

### F10. Tile-shape-scarcity tracking beyond raw counting — **COLLIDES with killed "deck-graded closure probability"**

### F11. Permanent-stranding blocks on early/mid-size features — **COLLIDES-WITH-CAVEAT on killed "targeted denial"**
**Mechanism:** several guides frame the top blocking tactic as making a feature *literally unfinishable forever*, but the examples given target early/mid-size, far-from-complete features, not near-complete large cities.
**Sources:** boostyourplay.com — "strand your opponents' Meeples on the board then they'll be virtually paralyzed"; tistaminis.com/blogs/blog/carcassonne-board-game-rules-strategy-expansions (via WebSearch synthesis).

These sources describe blocking *early/mid-size* features instead — worth one line in DECISIONS.md confirming the killed test's scope (near-complete + large) doesn't cover this variant, but I would not prioritize re-testing this without a stronger source; the strategic logic (permanently stranding a meeple early costs the opponent far more remaining-game value than squeezing an 80%-done city) is plausible but unverified here.

### F12. Meeple-in-reserve as offense-side optionality — **likely COLLIDES with killed "category-convex meeple lock-up"**
**Mechanism:** the value of *not yet* having placed a meeple (flexibility to grab a high-value tile, especially monasteries, the moment it appears) is repeatedly named across guides as a top consideration, independent of any penalty on stranded/locked meeples.
**Sources:** tilelord.com ("Try to always have at least one meeple in reserve"); thegamersguides.com; kingpandagames.com ("keep one guy back for the simple and quick... opportunities that will arise").
**Credibility:** blog-guide consensus (3+ independent sources) — one of the most repeated claims in the whole scan.
**NEW vs killed list:** **COLLIDES (likely).** This reads as the same territory as "category-convex meeple lock-up," but from the *offense* framing (value of held-back optionality) rather than a penalty term — worth a one-line check against the exact killed-lever spec (results.csv / DECISIONS.md) to confirm the offense-side framing wasn't a distinct untested formulation before fully discarding.

### F13. Finishing a small/cheap opponent feature purely for tempo — **COLLIDES-WITH-CAVEAT on killed "targeted denial"**
**Mechanism:** if a tile you'd place anyway happens to close a small opponent city/road, do it — denies their future expansion at zero cost to your own tempo, distinct from spending a turn specifically to squeeze a large near-complete city.
**Source:** attributed via WebSearch synthesis to a myboardgameguides.com-family strategy guide (not independently WebFetch-verified verbatim — lower confidence on exact wording than the other findings).
**Credibility:** weak — WebSearch-snippet only, exact source page not directly fetched/confirmed.
**NEW vs killed list:** **COLLIDES-WITH-CAVEAT**, same family as F11: the killed lever targeted *near-complete large* cities specifically; this is about *small/cheap* ones. Given the weak sourcing, I would not prioritize this over F1–F3, F6, F9.

### F14. Geometric multi-side farm siege (farming a city complex from 2–3 field directions) — **UNCLEAR mechanics**
**Mechanism:** claimed as "a valid technique to try and 'farm' a city from two or maybe even 3 sides," positioning farmers to touch the same city cluster from multiple separate field regions.
**Source:** chessandpoker.com / corroborating BGG snippet (via WebSearch, not independently verified).
**Credibility:** weak, single/ambiguous sourcing, and the underlying rules mechanics are unclear to me on inspection — a single field already gets full credit for every completed city on its border regardless of "how many sides" touch it, so the practical benefit may really be about *split-resistance* (holding the same city's value across multiple separate field claims so that no single dividing tile can cut you off from all of it) rather than literally "farming from more sides." Flagged UNCLEAR pending mechanical verification rather than dismissed outright.
**NEW vs killed list:** not directly comparable to anything on the killed list; low priority given the sourcing/mechanics uncertainty.
**Test (if pursued):** check whether the AI's farm placements optimize for split-resistance (count of tiles that would fragment the field into a losing piece) versus a naive city-adjacency-count-only valuation — this is the testable core if the "multi-side" framing turns out to just be folk-phrasing for split-resistance.

### F15 (external validation, not a new mechanism). Academic MCTS-vs-heuristic-search comparison broadly corroborates the project's own root-cause hypothesis
**Finding:** Ameneyro, Galván & Kuri Morales, "Playing Carcassonne with Monte Carlo Tree Search" (arXiv:2009.12974, IEEE SSCI 2020) compares vanilla MCTS / MCTS-RAVE against Star2.5 (search + domain-specific heuristic, architecturally similar in spirit to our search+hand-tuned-leaf design) and reports MCTS-family methods "consistently outperformed" the heuristic-search baseline, attributed to MCTS's ability to "find and follow long-term strategies" versus the heuristic's more myopic per-position scoring.
**Credibility:** peer-reviewed (IEEE SSCI 2020).
**NEW vs killed list:** **N/A — this is a restatement/external corroboration**, not a new lever. It doesn't identify a specific exploitable mechanism, but it is independent literature-level support for the project's already-documented root cause (docs/research/foundational_audit_2026-06-02.md — hand-crafted leaf caps learned strength). Cite as supporting context, not as new evidence to act on directly.
